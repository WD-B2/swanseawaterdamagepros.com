The leaks that start behind the appliance

Appliance leaks have a nasty habit of staying out of sight. A washing machine hose that weeps behind the unit, a dishwasher door gasket that drips under the cabinet, or the ice maker line to a refrigerator that sprays inside the wall can run for days before a stain or a smell gives it away. By then the water has been working into subfloor, cabinet bases and drywall.

When a washer or dishwasher floods the floor

A failed hose or valve can keep pumping while you sleep or are out of the house. Close the shut-off valve behind or beside the appliance, unplug it if the floor is dry around the plug and call. We answer after hours and on weekends.

Common culprits

Washing machine hoses

Rubber supply hoses bulge and burst. Braided steel hoses last longer but still age out. A burst hose can discharge while the machine is not even running.

Dishwasher connections

Supply lines, drain hoses and door seals leak into the cabinet base, and the damage spreads under adjacent flooring.

Refrigerator ice maker lines

Thin plastic or copper tubing cracks behind the unit, and the water runs along the floor or down into the basement.

Drain overflows

Clogged laundry standpipes and condensate pans spill gray water that carries detergent and lint.

Shut off and secure

Most appliances have their own shutoff valve at the wall, either under the sink or behind the unit. Close it first. If you cannot reach it, close the main valve. Unplug the appliance only if the cord and outlet are dry and you are standing on a dry surface. Then move the appliance away from the wall if it is safe to do so, so we can see where the water went.

Second floor laundry and the ceilings below

Many south coast homes have laundry on the upper floor, which looks convenient until a hose fails. The water follows the supply wall, drips through the ceiling below and settles in light fixtures. Do not turn on a ceiling light that has water in the fixture. Our work on a wet ceiling and wall cavity often goes hand in hand with an upstairs laundry leak.

What the job involves

  • Removal of standing water from floors and cabinet bases
  • Moisture mapping behind and below the appliance
  • Drying of subfloor, cabinet toe-kicks and adjacent walls
  • Removal of wet drywall, padding or flooring that will not dry
  • Clear documentation of the source, the path and the readings

Insurance and the appliance itself

Sudden discharge from an appliance is the kind of loss many homeowners policies address, though not always the appliance or a long-running leak. The Massachusetts Division of Insurance recommends reading your policy and reporting a loss promptly. A slow, hidden leak is treated differently from a sudden failure, so describe what you found and when. We keep the record of what we measured.

Cut the risk of the next one

  • Replace rubber washer hoses with braided steel ones and check them each year
  • Turn off the washer supply valves when you travel
  • Put a drain pan under a washer on an upper floor
  • Look behind the refrigerator when you move it for cleaning
  • Do not run a dishwasher or washer while the house is empty

When the leak was slow

A slow leak behind an appliance can be weeks old before it shows. In that case, expect rusted fasteners, a stained subfloor and possibly growth on the back of drywall. We open what we have to open and explain whether the damage is limited to a small area or has spread. A moisture reading and a look behind the unit usually settle the question.

If the leak came from the kitchen, our kitchen water damage page covers cabinets and flooring in more detail.

What the standard says about gray water

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the industry reference for classifying water by contamination. Water from a supply line starts clean, while water from a washer or dishwasher drain is treated as gray. That classification is why we ask which appliance leaked and which line it was.

What to do before help arrives

  • Close the appliance shutoff valve or the main valve
  • Do not touch a plug or outlet if the floor around it is wet
  • Do not run the appliance again until a technician has looked at it
  • Pull the appliance away from the wall only if it is safe and you are standing on a dry surface
  • Photograph the connections and the water trail
