Why air conditioners leak indoors

An air conditioner pulls moisture out of the air as it cools. That moisture, called condensate, drips into a pan and flows out through a drain line. When the line clogs with algae and dust, or the pan rusts through, the water goes where gravity sends it. Air handlers in attics and closets send it into the ceiling below. Basement units send it across the slab.

Summers on the south coast are humid, and a system that runs hard through July and August produces a lot of condensate. A drain line that was fine in June may be fully clogged by August.

Signs worth acting on

  • A brown stain or damp patch on a ceiling beneath an attic air handler
  • Water in the drain pan or around the base of the indoor unit
  • A musty smell near vents that gets worse when the system runs
  • Rust on the air handler cabinet or the pan
  • A float switch that has shut the system off

Turn the system off first

If the unit is leaking, switch the thermostat to off and shut the breaker for the air handler. Continuing to run the system adds water every minute. Then call an HVAC technician to clear the line or replace the pan. Because the technician works on the system and we work on the building, the two jobs do not overlap.

What gets wet and how we dry it

AC leaks are usually slow, which sounds better than it is. Slow leaks soak insulation, rust metal and keep drywall damp for weeks. The Massachusetts Department of Public Health notes that relative humidity above about 70 percent for a few days can support mold growth, and a leaking air handler in an attic or closet can hold a pocket of moisture at exactly that level.

We use moisture meters and a thermal camera to find the wet section, then remove what will not dry and set equipment.

Claims for a slow, ongoing condensate leak can be treated differently from a sudden one, so describe the timeline honestly to your carrier.

Keep it from happening again

  • Have a technician flush the condensate line at the start of every cooling season
  • Install a float switch or a secondary drain pan under attic units
  • Look at the pan and line each month in summer
  • Replace old units that show rust on the pan or cabinet
  • Keep air filters changed so coils stay cleaner

Roof and plumbing leaks look similar from below, so see our ceiling water damage page if the source is unclear.

Where the industry standard fits

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the published industry reference for professional water damage restoration. It treats slow condensate leaks like this one as a classification problem, so the first question is how long the material has been wet. That question is why we ask when you first noticed the stain.

What to do and what not to do before help arrives

  • Turn the thermostat off and switch off the breaker for the air handler
  • Do not run the system while water is dripping from it
  • Do not touch wiring or a wet air handler cabinet
  • Place a bucket under a drip and keep people out from under a bulging ceiling
  • Take photos of the stain, the pan and the drain line
