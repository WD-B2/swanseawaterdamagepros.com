How water changes a wood floor

Hardwood takes on water through its bottom and edges and expands. As the bottom of a board swells faster than the top, the edges rise and the board cups. If the top dries faster than the bottom, or the floor is sanded too early, the center can rise instead. That is called crowning. Both are signs of moisture imbalance, and neither goes away by waiting.

Cupping

Board edges rise higher than the center, usually after water from below or from a long leak.

Crowning

The center of a board rises, often after drying and sanding done too early.

Buckling

Boards lift off the subfloor in sections after heavy, sudden water.

Staining

Dark gray or black marks show water has sat in the wood and finish for a long time.

Why the first days matter

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health notes that absorbent materials not dried within 24 to 48 hours often need replacement. Wood is not as fast to ruin as carpet padding, but a floor that stays wet for days can mold underneath, stain and lose its fit. Fast, controlled drying gives boards the best chance of returning to flat.

Drying a hardwood floor

1

Find where the water went

Moisture meters check boards, the subfloor and the area under cabinets and walls.

2

Remove standing water

Wet vacuums remove surface water, and furniture comes off the floor.

3

Dry from the top and below

Drying mats, panels or special equipment pull moisture through the boards. Air movers and dehumidifiers dry the room.

4

Wait for stable readings

Boards need to reach a normal moisture level and rest before anyone decides on sanding or replacement.

Sand and refinish, or replace?

Light cupping often relaxes once boards are dry and a flooring contractor can sand and refinish. Severe buckling, widespread staining or boards that have separated usually mean replacement of those sections. A decision made too early is the most common mistake, because boards keep moving as they dry. We tell you what readings say and when a flooring contractor can evaluate.

Common causes in local homes

Older Swansea homes often have hardwood over a crawl space or basement. A leak underneath, humid summers or a plumbing failure above can all wet it. Kitchen floors suffer from sink and dishwasher leaks, and floors over a damp crawl space can cup without any visible puddle.

What you can do now

  • Remove rugs, mats and furniture from the wet area
  • Wipe standing water with towels but do not scrub
  • Do not cover the floor with plastic or wax
  • Take photos of cupping and staining
  • Leave the heat on to a normal level, and do not crank it

Species, finish and subfloor all matter

Oak, maple and pine move differently, and wide boards move more than narrow ones. Engineered hardwood behaves differently again because its layers respond to moisture in different directions. A floor nailed to a plank subfloor releases water differently from one glued to a slab. We note the species and construction when we can, because they affect how long drying takes and what a flooring contractor will recommend afterward.

Insurance often covers sudden water losses, and an adjuster may ask for readings. See structural drying for how we measure.

Drying wood by the standard

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the published industry reference for water damage restoration, and it describes drying wood floors toward a stable moisture content before any decision on sanding or replacement. That is why we wait for stable readings.

What to do and what not to do now

  • Do not cover the floor with plastic, wax or rugs
  • Do not sand or refinish until readings are stable
  • Do not run a heater at full power to rush the drying
  • Remove furniture and wipe standing water with towels
  • Photograph cupping and staining as it develops
