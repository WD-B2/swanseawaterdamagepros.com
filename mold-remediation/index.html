A musty smell is a moisture problem first

Most people call about mold after a smell, a stain or visible spots on a wall, ceiling or basement. Mold is a symptom of water that stayed too long. The Massachusetts Department of Public Health says moisture is the cause of mold, that growth can begin when relative humidity stays above about 70 percent for a few days, and that absorbent materials not dried within 24 to 48 hours often need replacement.

  • Dark or fuzzy spots on drywall, ceilings or baseboards
  • A musty smell that gets worse in damp weather
  • Peeling paint or wallpaper near a past leak
  • Stains that return after cleaning
  • A basement or crawl space that stays damp

Testing, and what the state says

Homeowners ask whether they should test the air. The Massachusetts Department of Public Health does not recommend routine mold testing, because there are no standards to compare results against. The practical question is where the moisture is and what materials are affected. We find those answers with meters, thermal imaging and a careful look.

How remediation works

1

Fix the moisture first

Remediation fails if the source is still active. A plumber, roofer or drainage contractor fixes it.

2

Contain the area

Plastic sheeting and negative air keep disturbed material from spreading to the rest of the house.

3

Remove affected materials

Drywall, insulation, carpet and other porous materials with growth are removed and bagged.

4

Clean what remains

Framing and hard surfaces are cleaned with HEPA vacuums and wiped.

5

Dry and verify

Equipment dries the area. Readings confirm it before rebuild.

Why containment matters

Scrubbing mold off a wall with a brush and a spray bottle can spread it. Containment keeps the work area separate from the rest of the home, and HEPA air filtration captures fine particles. Doing the work without these steps can move the problem from one room to another. That is the main reason we recommend leaving significant growth to trained crews.

Where mold shows up in local homes

Basements near the bay, crawl spaces with dirt floors, bathrooms with weak ventilation and ceilings under roof leaks are common sites. It also follows incomplete drying after pipe failures, basement floods and AC leaks. Learn more about prevention on our structural drying page.

Licensing and qualifications

Rules about mold work vary from state to state, so ask whether your state licenses mold work and what that license covers. Ask any contractor you consider what standard they follow, request the scope in writing and compare scopes between companies before you decide.

Preparing for a visit

Before we arrive, note where you see or smell the problem, when any leak happened and whether the area has been cleaned or painted. Photos help. Keep the HVAC system off if the problem is near a return vent, and avoid disturbing visible growth. Tell us about any pets, young children or household members who are concerned, so we can plan containment and access. Written scopes and the plan for drying should be clear before any removal starts.

We do not make claims about health effects. If you or someone in your home is concerned about symptoms, speak with a doctor. Our job is to find the moisture, contain the growth, remove affected materials and dry the building.

The standard behind testing and remediation

Mold testing and remediation follow the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, a published industry document. It covers assessing the moisture source, containing the work area, removing or cleaning affected material and verifying the result. Ask any company you are considering whether its crews work to S520 and how they document it. We do not make health claims about mold, and questions about symptoms belong with your physician.
