Save Photos and Documents After Water Damage in Swansea, MA

Paper and photos can often be saved if you act within a day or two. Here is how to handle them, what to avoid and what to freeze for later.

Act fast, handle gently

Wet paper is fragile, and wet photographs stick to anything they touch. The first goal is to stop them from drying in a clump. Do not pull stuck pages apart. Do not use heat. Gently lift items from the water with both hands and set them on a clean, flat surface. If you cannot dry things properly within about 48 hours, freezing is the way to buy time.

What you can air dry

  • Single sheets of paper, laid flat on a clean towel or paper without ink
  • Photographs that are not stuck together, face up on a clean surface
  • Books that are only damp, stood on end with pages fanned
  • Important documents you can photocopy first
  • Certificates and records that are not fragile

What to freeze

Bundles of wet papers, soaked books, photo albums and prints that have started to stick are better frozen than dried in place. Put them in freezer bags, pressing out air, and place them in a freezer. Freezing stops mold and pauses damage until a conservator or a document drying service can help. Do not freeze items with wet, flaking paint, such as paintings, or items that need special handling.

Photographs

  • Rinse mud off gently with clean water, holding the photo by the edges
  • Dry face up on a clean, absorbent surface
  • Separate photos from albums and frames while wet, using care
  • Do not use paper towels that can stick to the emulsion
  • Freeze photos that have stuck together

Digital copies are the best protection. If you have scanned originals, wet prints matter less. If not, scanning the survivors as soon as they are dry gives you a second chance next time.

Important documents

Passports, birth certificates, deeds, titles and tax records can sometimes be replaced, and sometimes not. Dry them flat and make copies. If a document is badly damaged, contact the issuing agency about a replacement. Keep a list of what was damaged and what you replaced. These records may also support an insurance claim for the loss.

Where you store things matters

Basements and attics are the usual places water gets to boxes of papers. Store important records in waterproof bins on shelves, or keep copies offsite or in the cloud. A scan of every important document and a few photos of the family archive is cheap insurance. It also makes a claim easier because it shows what you owned.

While the structure dries

Our crew focuses on removing water and drying the building. We will tell you what is in the affected area and what can be moved out first, and we will not throw out your papers. If a leak came through a ceiling onto a desk or filing cabinet, see our ceiling water damage page, and for basement storage, see basement water damage cleanup.

Making a recovery space

Set up a clean table, ideally in a dry room away from the work area, with blotting paper, clean towels and a roll of freezer bags. Work with clean hands and, if the water was dirty, wear gloves. Label bags by room or box so you know what came from where. For large archives, work in batches, freezing what you cannot get to right away.

If you have items of real value, such as old family photographs or legal records, write down what they are before they go into the freezer so you can find them later. It is slow work, but it is the part of a water loss where your effort most clearly changes the outcome.

Digitize as you go

As items dry, photograph or scan them. A phone camera in good light is enough for most documents. Save the files in two places, such as your phone and a cloud account. Even if a page is stained or faded, a digital copy preserves the information. Name the files clearly so you can find them in a year, and keep a short list of what you saved and what was lost.

Primary-source context

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance advises homeowners to report claims promptly and read their policies carefully. Documents that show what you owned and what was damaged help support a claim.

Published guidance on wet records

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the industry reference for water damage restoration, and it notes that contents such as paper and photographs need their own handling. Freezing wet paper is a widely used way to pause damage until it can be dried properly.
