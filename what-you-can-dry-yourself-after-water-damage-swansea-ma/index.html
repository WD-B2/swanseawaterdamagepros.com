What You Can Dry Yourself vs What Needs a Pro, Swansea MA

A small, clean spill on a hard floor is a DIY job. Anything larger, wetter or dirtier usually is not. Here is how to tell which one you have, and what to do first either way.

The honest answer

You can dry a small amount of clean water that has not soaked into anything. A cup of water on a tile floor, a damp towel under a sink or a drip from a window sill all qualify. What you cannot do well with towels and a fan is dry water that has gotten into carpet padding, drywall, insulation or the layers under a floor. Those materials hold water you cannot see, and drying them takes measurement and equipment.

The 24 to 48 hour rule

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health notes that absorbent materials not dried within 24 to 48 hours often need to be replaced. It also says mold growth can begin when relative humidity stays above about 70 percent for a few days. That rule drives the decision. If you can fully dry the area inside that window, DIY might work. If you cannot be sure, call.

Safe for most people to do

  • Wipe up small spills on hard, sealed surfaces right away
  • Run a dehumidifier and a fan in a room with a minor, clean leak
  • Pull a wet rug or mat outside to dry
  • Lift wet cardboard and paper off floors
  • Stop the source and take photos for your records

Where DIY often fails

Carpet and padding

The padding holds far more water than the carpet and dries slowly underneath. See carpet water damage restoration.

Walls

Water wicks up drywall and stays inside the cavity. See wall and drywall repair.

Hardwood

Boards cup and crown if drying is uneven. See hardwood floor water damage repair.

Anything with dirty water

Flood and sewer water are contaminated and should not be handled casually.

When to stop and call

  • Water has been present for more than a day
  • The water came from a drain, a toilet, a flood or the outdoors
  • Walls, ceilings or insulation are wet
  • The floor feels soft, springy or looks like it is lifting
  • You smell a musty odor that returns after drying
  • You cannot find where the water is coming from

How a professional measures what you cannot see

Moisture meters read the water content of wood and drywall. A thermal camera shows cool, wet areas behind a surface. We also take readings on unaffected parts of the house to compare. That is what tells us a wall is dry. A wall that feels dry to the touch and reads wet to a meter is the reason many do-it-yourself jobs end in mold or rot weeks later.

Using a rental dehumidifier

If you do decide to dry a small area yourself, a dehumidifier does more than an open window, especially in humid weather. Close doors and windows in the room, run the unit continuously, empty the tank regularly and use a fan to move air across the wet surface. Keep checking after a few days with a moisture meter if you can borrow one, and do not stop because the surface feels dry. If anything still reads high, call us.

What a successful DIY dry-out looks like

A good result is one where the surface is dry, the smell is gone and a meter or a careful touch of the baseboard shows no dampness a few days later. A bad result is one where the surface looks dry but the room feels clammy, the baseboard paint bubbles or the odor returns on humid days.

If you see those signs, the water is still inside and the 48 hour clock has run out. At that point the helpful step is to call, not to run another fan.

What state guidance says

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health says moisture is the cause of mold. It states that growth can begin after relative humidity stays above about 70 percent for a few days and that absorbent materials not dried within 24 to 48 hours often need to be replaced. Use those numbers as your clock.

Near Mount Hope Bay and the river corridors, outdoor humidity stays high through summer, which makes DIY drying slower than in a dry climate. That is one reason Swansea homeowners with larger leaks often cannot meet the 48 hour window by themselves.

If your house is in Dighton or Rehoboth, where well and septic systems add other sources of water, the same rule applies. When the leak is large, our water extraction page explains the first step.

The industry reference

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the published reference for professional water damage restoration. It is built on measuring moisture instead of guessing, which is the main thing a professional adds to a dry-out.

Local questions, answered

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health notes 24 to 48 hours as a key window for absorbent materials. If you are past that or unsure, call now.

A wet and dry vacuum can handle a small spill. It will not dry what soaked into the walls or floor.

In humid weather it can make things worse. A dehumidifier and closed windows usually work better.

A fan helps move air once water has been extracted. Do not run electrical equipment through standing water.

Many policies require reasonable steps to prevent more damage. Ask your carrier if you are unsure.
