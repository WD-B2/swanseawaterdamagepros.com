How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Swansea, MA

A scope of work is the document that says what will be done to your house. It is dense, but a few minutes of careful reading can save weeks of confusion.

What a scope of work is

A scope of work is a written description of the work a contractor will do. In a water damage job it covers the areas affected, the steps to remove water, the materials to be removed or cleaned, the equipment to be placed and the documentation you will receive. It is the document you refer back to if something is disputed later. If your contractor has not provided one, ask for it before work begins.

Read it in this order

1

Scope summary

Check that the source, the date and the affected rooms are right.

2

Removal items

See what is being taken out and why, with locations and sizes.

3

Drying items

Look at the equipment, the number of units and the monitoring plan.

4

Cleaning items

See what is cleaned, treated or disposed of.

5

Exclusions and assumptions

Read what is not included and what could change the scope.

Measurements and quantities

A scope often lists areas in square feet and linear feet, equipment in units or days and materials by type. Check that the numbers match what you see. If it says 200 square feet of drywall and the wet area is a small closet, ask why. If it lists twelve air movers for a small room, ask how that was calculated. Good contractors can explain their numbers.

Red flags

  • No mention of moisture readings or a dry standard
  • Pressure to sign immediately
  • Removal of materials that are not wet or not described
  • Vague language such as miscellaneous or as needed
  • No exclusions listed
  • No mention of documentation for your claim

Changes after the scope is signed

Scopes change when hidden damage is found. A change order, a written update to the scope, is the right way to handle it. You should know about the change and the reason before the work proceeds. If a contractor expands the job without telling you, that is a problem worth raising right away. Read about the way we handle changes on our water extraction page and our mold remediation page.

Share the scope with your carrier

Your insurance carrier will likely want to see the scope, the photos and the readings. Share them early. If the carrier's estimate differs from the contractor's, ask both to explain the difference line by line. Differences are common, and they are often about which materials need removal.

Questions to ask when you sit down with the scope

Bring a pen and ask the contractor to walk you through each section in person or on the phone. Ask which items are firm and which are estimates. Ask how the contractor will tell you when a drying goal has been met, and what happens if it takes longer than planned. Ask whether pictures will be taken at each stage, and where the readings will be stored.

Ask what happens to your belongings if they need to be moved. Write the answers in the margin or in an email so you have a record. A contractor who welcomes these questions is usually one who plans carefully.

If you share the scope with a second company for an opinion, remove the prices first if you prefer an unbiased view of the steps. The comparison of steps tells you whether two plans are even covering the same work.

Primary-source reminders

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance advises homeowners to read their policy and report claims promptly. It also notes that sewer backup and sump pump failure are excluded by default unless an endorsement is added, which affects how a sewage or basement scope will be treated.

The Swansea Building Department requires a permit for any installation or repair of plumbing and asks homeowners to check with the department about inspections. A repair scope that includes plumbing, electrical or mechanical work should say who obtains the permit.

For homes in Somerset or Dighton, the structure of a scope is the same, and our crew will go through ours with you before starting.

What to do before you sign

  • Ask for the scope in writing and read the exclusions first
  • Do not sign a blank or partly filled form
  • Do not pay a large amount up front
  • Ask who will pull permits for repair work
  • Keep a copy of everything you sign
