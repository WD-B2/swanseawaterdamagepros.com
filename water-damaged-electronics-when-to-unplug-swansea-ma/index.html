Water Damaged Electronics: When to Unplug in Swansea, MA

Water and electricity do not mix, and the order of operations matters. Here is when to cut power, what to touch and what to leave alone after a leak or flood.

Safety first, electronics second

Your safety matters more than any device. Do not touch electrical equipment, cords or outlets while standing in water or on a wet floor. If water reaches the panel, a sub-panel or wall outlets, leave the area and call your electric utility or an electrician. If you can reach the main breaker without standing in water, switching off power to the affected area is sensible. If you cannot, do not try.

Unplugging, step by step

1

Check the floor

Only approach if the floor and the outlet are dry.

2

Cut power at the breaker if safe

Turning off the circuit is safer than touching a wet plug.

3

Unplug with a dry hand

Pull the plug, not the cord, and keep your other hand away from metal.

4

Move the device to a dry area

Do not turn it on to test it.

Do not try to turn it on

The most common mistake is plugging in a wet device to see if it still works. If water is inside, powering it up can short circuits and turn a repairable device into a total loss. Let it sit unplugged. For phones and laptops, remove batteries and cases if you can, and keep them in a dry place. Do not use a hair dryer, an oven or a microwave. Heat can damage components and push water further inside.

What can often be saved

Phones and tablets

Power off, remove the case and let them dry in open air. Do not charge them until they are fully dry. A technician can check for corrosion.

Laptops

Unplug, remove the battery if possible and call a repair shop. Corrosion can develop within days.

Televisions and monitors

Leave them unplugged for several days and ask a technician before turning them on.

Appliances

Refrigerators, washers and dryers should be checked by a technician if they sat in water.

What to keep for the record

Take photos of each device, its model number and where it was when the water arrived. Keep damaged items until the adjuster has seen them unless they are unsafe. A list of the electronics, with approximate purchase dates and costs, helps support your claim.

Wiring, outlets and the panel

Water inside wall cavities can reach wiring and outlets. Even after the room is dry, an electrician should inspect any outlets or switches that were wet. We open walls, dry the cavity and document readings, but we do not do electrical repair. See our wall and drywall page for what happens in the cavity, and basement water damage cleanup for a typical electrical risk area.

Storm outages and surge

During a storm, power can go out and come back with a surge. Unplug sensitive electronics before a storm if you can, and use a surge protector with a good rating. If the power comes back while your basement is wet, avoid using the equipment down there until it is checked.

Chargers, power strips and extension cords

Small items cause real hazards. A power strip that sat in water should be thrown away, not dried and reused. Extension cords with damaged insulation can shock, and chargers can fail. Anything with exposed wire or a swollen plug is not worth saving. Replace them with new ones, and plug the new ones in only when the area is completely dry.

Check surge protectors for water inside and replace them too. These items are cheap, which makes the risk of keeping them hard to justify.

Space heaters, dehumidifiers and shop vacuums that were in water need a technician before reuse. Do not plug them in to test them, since a short can start a fire.

Local conditions that raise the risk

The Swansea hazard mitigation report records March 2010 floods in which residents lost furnaces and water heaters. Those are electrical and gas appliances that sit low in many homes, so a flooded basement is also an electrical hazard.

In Rehoboth, the town's own planning history notes that in March 2018 about 85 percent of the town lost power and 32 roads closed, and the fire department pumped out houses. Power loss and flooding at the same time means pumps stop and dehumidifiers go off. Ask an electrician before restoring power to a flooded area.

Homeowners in Rehoboth and Somerset see these conditions in storm season, and so do many in Swansea. If the water came through a ceiling, see ceiling water damage repair.

Safety references

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the industry reference for water damage restoration and it warns about electrical hazards in wet buildings. The National Electrical Code, known as NEC, is the reference electricians follow when wiring is inspected or replaced after a flood.
