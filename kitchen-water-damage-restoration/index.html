Kitchens hide leaks well

Kitchens contain a lot of water and a lot of places for it to hide. A slow drip under the sink soaks the cabinet floor. A dishwasher hose leaks into the toe-kick space and runs under the flooring. The refrigerator ice maker line feeds a damp spot behind the unit. By the time you notice a smell or a soft spot in the floor, the damage has often spread.

Under the sink

Supply lines, shutoffs and drain traps leak into the cabinet base, and particleboard swells quickly.

Dishwasher

The door gasket, supply line or drain hose leaks and water travels under adjacent cabinets.

Refrigerator line

The water supply tube behind the unit can crack and spray against the wall or floor.

Leaking above

A bathroom or laundry on the floor above sends water through the kitchen ceiling.

What happens to cabinets and flooring

Solid wood cabinets usually dry. Particleboard and MDF absorb water, swell and lose strength. Laminate flooring and engineered boards buckle at the seams. Tile and vinyl may survive, while the subfloor underneath stays wet. We measure and tell you which parts can stay and which are at risk. Our hardwood floor page covers kitchen hardwood in more detail.

Drying a kitchen without tearing it apart

The goal is to dry as much as possible in place. Small holes drilled in toe-kicks let air and heat get behind cabinets. Drying mats or panels dry floors. Air movers and dehumidifiers pull moisture out of the room. Removal happens only where drying will not work, such as soaked insulation, swollen cabinet bases or drywall that has lost strength.

Why quick action saves money

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health notes that materials which stay wet more than 24 to 48 hours often need to be replaced. In a kitchen, that can mean the difference between drying a cabinet and replacing it. Prompt calls limit what gets thrown away.

Repairs and permits

Counters, appliances and the cost conversation

Water behind a kitchen often means moving appliances, disconnecting the dishwasher and sometimes pulling a toe-kick or a section of cabinet. A plumber disconnects and reconnects lines, and a countertop installer handles any that must be lifted. We coordinate the drying sequence with those trades so nothing is reinstalled over a wet floor. Costs depend on cabinet material, how much floor is affected and how much has to come out, and we explain those factors before work starts.

Living in the house while it dries

A kitchen loss does not stop the household from eating. Plan for a temporary setup in another room with a microwave, a kettle and a cooler, and keep food and dishes away from the work area. Drying equipment is loud, so expect noise at night. Keep pets and children away from cords and equipment. If the stove or oven has to be moved, ask the installer about gas connections before anyone touches them.

Swansea requires a permit for plumbing repair and replacement, which usually applies to a leaking sink supply or a new dishwasher line. A plumber and a cabinet or flooring contractor take over once the room is dry. Related topics include appliance leak water damage and wall and drywall repair.

The standard behind the method

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the industry reference for water damage restoration. Drying cabinets and subfloor in place, with measured readings, is the kind of practice it describes.

What to do before help arrives

  • Shut off the supply to the sink or appliance, or close the main valve
  • Do not use a dishwasher or disposal that is leaking
  • Do not stand in water near outlets or the stove
  • Empty the cabinet under the sink so the base can dry
  • Photograph the leak, the cabinet and the floor
