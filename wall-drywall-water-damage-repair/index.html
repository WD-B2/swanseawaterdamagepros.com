Signs the wall is wet

  • Paint that bubbles, blisters or peels
  • Drywall that is soft, crumbles or has a bowed surface
  • A stain that grows or comes back after it is painted
  • Baseboards that swell or pull away
  • A musty smell near the wall

Because walls close over the problem, moisture meters and thermal cameras do the finding. A wall can look fine and still hold a wet cavity.

Drywall, plaster and what they do when wet

Drywall is gypsum between paper. Soaked drywall loses strength and can grow mold on its paper facing. Plaster over lath, common in older Swansea houses, handles water differently. It can dry but may crack or release from the lath. The approach changes with the material, and the age of the house matters here.

The flood cut

When a wall has taken water from the floor up, such as after a flood or a basement leak, the wet portion is cut out at a height above the water line, often two feet or more. The cut exposes the cavity, lets insulation come out and lets the framing dry. It is a straight, clean cut that a drywall contractor can patch. Not every wet wall needs one. If readings show the wall can dry in place, it stays.

What causes wall damage

Supply line failures, roof leaks and storm water all end up in walls. Our pages on burst pipes, ceiling leaks and flooding describe how each sends water into the cavity. Wall damage from the floor up is common with basement water.

Moisture and mold

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health says moisture is the cause of mold growth and that testing is generally not recommended because no standards exist to compare results against. The practical step is to find and remove the moisture. If growth is visible on drywall paper or framing, mold remediation is a separate process.

Insulation, electrical and baseboards

Wall cavities hold more than drywall. Fiberglass insulation sags when wet and holds water against framing. Electrical boxes and wiring may be in the wet area, and an electrician should check them before anything is closed up. Baseboards and trim are often saved if removed carefully and dried, though particleboard trim rarely survives. Each of these decisions comes down to readings and what the materials are made of, and we explain our reasoning along the way.

Older plaster walls, such as those in the village homes of Hortonville, are measured first so that only what has to be removed is removed.

Why paint and primer come last

Fresh paint over a damp wall peels within weeks and can hide a leak that is still active. A wall should read dry in several places and stay dry across a few days before patching starts. Stain-blocking primer helps with water rings, but it does not fix moisture. Waiting for good readings is cheaper than painting twice, and it keeps the repair from trapping dampness inside the cavity.

Patching drywall does not usually need a permit, but electrical, plumbing and insulation work inside the wall can. Ask the contractor, and note that Swansea's building department asks homeowners to check with them about required inspections.

What to do and what not to do first

  • Do not paint or caulk over a wet wall
  • Do not cut into a wall near wiring or pipes yourself
  • Keep furniture and curtains away from the wet area
  • Turn off power to the room if the wall is dripping near outlets
  • Photograph the stain and its edges so changes are visible
