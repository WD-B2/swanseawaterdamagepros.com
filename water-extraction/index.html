Why standing water needs to come out first

Water that sits in a building keeps spreading. It wicks up drywall, soaks into the bottom plate of a wall and finds its way under flooring. In a coastal town like Swansea, where the ground water is already close to the surface in many neighborhoods, a basement or slab that takes on water can refill itself if the source is not dealt with. Extraction is the step that stops the spread and gives every later step a fair start.

Extraction is not the same thing as drying. Pumps and wet vacuums remove bulk water, but the moisture that has soaked into framing, subfloor and plaster stays behind. That is why a proper job moves straight from removal into measurement and drying. Our structural drying work picks up where the extraction ends.

Why extraction cannot wait for morning

Standing water starts moving into baseboards, drywall and flooring within hours. The line is staffed overnight, on weekends and on holidays, so you can describe the situation while the water is still sitting on the surface and get a plan for the next step.

What extraction looks like on site

1

Stop the source

If a pipe, appliance or supply line is the cause, the water is shut off first. If the water is coming in from outside, we note where and how it is entering.

2

Make the space safe

Standing water and live electrical circuits do not mix. We look for submerged outlets, panels and appliances before anyone wades in.

3

Remove the bulk water

Submersible pumps handle deep water. Portable extraction units and wet vacuums handle shallow water on floors, carpet and hard surfaces.

4

Measure what remains

Moisture meters and a thermal camera show where water traveled beyond what is visible, so the drying plan matches the real wet area.

Where we see extraction jobs around Swansea

The southern end of Swansea, including the Grove, Touisset and the Cove Street area, has a long record of high water. The town hazard mitigation report describes homes that floated off their foundations in the 1954 hurricane and a March 2010 flood that closed schools and cut off emergency access. Those events are the extreme end, but the same geography means basements near Mount Hope Bay and the Lee and Palmer river corridors see water more often than hillside homes.

Most calls are smaller and closer to home: a supply line that let go while a family was out, a water heater tank that rusted through, or a washing machine hose that burst on the second floor and sent water down through two ceilings. The method is the same. Get the water out, find where it went and dry what can be saved.

What to do while you wait

  • Shut off the main water valve if a pipe or appliance is the source
  • Turn off power to the affected area at the panel only if you can reach it without standing in water
  • Lift small valuables, electronics and paperwork above the waterline
  • Take photos and video of the water and damaged items before moving anything
  • Open a door or window if the weather allows, but do not run fans on wet carpet yourself

If the water is deep enough to reach outlets, stay out and call us and your electric utility. Do not use a household vacuum on standing water.

Clean water, gray water and floodwater

Not all water is the same. Water from a supply line is generally clean at the start, but it picks up contaminants as it moves through a building and sits. Water from a washing machine or dishwasher drain is gray. Water that enters from outside, or backs up from a drain, is treated as contaminated from the first minute.

The category changes what gets removed and what gets cleaned, so we ask about the source on the phone. For the contaminated end of that range, see our pages on sewage backup cleanup and flood damage cleanup.

Extraction and your insurance claim

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance notes that standard homeowners policies exclude flood, and that seepage into a basement is generally excluded as well. Sudden discharge from plumbing or an appliance is a different situation and often covered. That difference matters for how you describe the loss, so give your carrier the facts as you know them and read your policy.

We document the water, the affected materials and the readings so the paperwork reflects what actually happened. We do not decide coverage.
