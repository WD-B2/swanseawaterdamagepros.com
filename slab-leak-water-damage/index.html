Signs a leak may be under your slab

A slab leak is a pipe leak in or beneath a concrete floor. You do not see it directly. You see what it does. A single warm spot on a floor, a water meter that keeps moving when everything is off, the sound of running water in a quiet house, or flooring that lifts, stains or smells musty are the common clues. A sudden bump in the water bill can tell the same story.

Warm or hot floor spots

A hot water line leaking under the slab warms the concrete above it.

A moving water meter

If the meter turns with every fixture and appliance off, water is escaping somewhere.

Running water sounds

A hiss or trickle with no fixture open can point to a pressurized line leaking.

Flooring changes

Laminate peels, tile grout darkens and carpet smells damp over the leak.

Who does what

A plumber or leak detection specialist locates and repairs the leak. That can involve acoustic listening, tracing the line and sometimes breaking through the slab or rerouting the pipe overhead. We do not locate or repair supply lines. We manage the water the leak has already released and the moisture trapped in the flooring, subfloor and walls above and beside it.

Because Swansea requires a permit for any installation or repair of plumbing, a repair that reroutes lines will involve the building department. Ask your plumber what applies before work begins.

When a slab leak turns urgent

Most slab leaks start small, but warm floors, a rising water bill or water showing at a baseboard mean the line is actively leaking. Close the main valve and call. The after-hours line can help you sort the drying side while a plumber handles detection and repair.

What water under a slab does

Concrete looks solid, but it is porous. Water moves through it and rises as vapor into flooring. Over time it can lift tile, warp hardwood and swell baseboards. In a house with a basement slab near the water table, as is common near Mount Hope Bay and the river corridors, it can also raise indoor humidity and leave a stale smell. Slab moisture is slow to disappear, which is why moisture readings matter more here than anywhere.

The damage side of a slab leak

1

Document the signs

Photos, meter readings and moisture readings establish when and where the leak affected the house.

2

Remove affected flooring

Wet carpet, padding or laminate is lifted so the slab can dry.

3

Dry the slab and walls

Dehumidifiers and air movers pull moisture from concrete and the lower walls. Slabs dry slowly and are monitored with repeat readings.

4

Verify before rebuild

Floor coverings go back only when readings confirm the slab and framing are dry.

Insurance considerations

Policies treat slab leaks differently from one carrier to the next, and some exclude the cost of reaching the pipe. The Massachusetts Division of Insurance advises reading the policy and reporting a loss promptly. Our records of readings and photos help describe the damage. We do not decide coverage.

What to tell the plumber and what to tell us

Give the plumber the dates of the bill increase, the location of any warm spots and the sound you hear. Give us the flooring type, the age of the slab and whether the area has been wet before. Together, the two pieces of information let each trade plan its work. If the leak is under a finished floor, a plumber may need access that affects flooring, which is why the order of work matters.

Industry standard context

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the industry reference for water damage restoration. It treats concrete as a slow-drying material that has to be measured, not assumed dry, which is why slab jobs rely on repeat readings.
