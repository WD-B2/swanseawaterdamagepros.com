Restoration vs Remediation Explained for Swansea, MA

Restoration, remediation, mitigation and repair sound alike, and companies use them loosely. Here is what each one usually means and why the difference matters when you read a quote.

Why the words matter

Contractors use several terms for work that follows water damage. Homeowners hear them interchangeably, which makes quotes hard to compare. Two companies can both say they will restore your home and mean very different things. Knowing what each term normally covers lets you ask better questions and spot gaps in a scope.

Mitigation

Mitigation is the emergency stage. It means stopping further damage by removing water, extracting standing water, setting up drying equipment and removing materials that cannot be saved. The goal is to limit the loss. Insurers use the term because policies often require you to mitigate, which means taking reasonable steps to prevent the damage from growing. Our water extraction page describes this stage.

Restoration

Restoration is a broad word. In the water damage industry it usually means returning a building to its pre-loss condition, which can include mitigation, drying, cleaning and sometimes rebuilding. Some companies use the word only for drying and cleaning, and others include construction. Ask what a company means when it says it will restore your home.

Remediation

Remediation is a term used mostly for removing a contaminant, such as mold, and fixing the conditions that allowed it. Mold remediation includes containment, removal of affected materials, cleaning and drying. The word suggests a plan with a defined end point rather than general repair. See mold remediation for how it works.

Repair and reconstruction

Repair and reconstruction mean putting the building back together after it is dry. That includes new drywall, paint, flooring, cabinetry and trim. It is construction, and it usually needs a general contractor or a set of trades. Some restoration companies do it, others do not. It should start only after readings show the structure is dry.

Questions to ask when you read a quote

  • Does the scope include drying with documented readings?
  • Is rebuilding included or separate?
  • If mold is present, is remediation part of the scope?
  • Who handles permits for repair work?
  • What happens if hidden damage is found after demolition?

Where insurance fits in

Insurers often split a loss into emergency services, drying, remediation and repair, and they may pay different parts at different times. The words on your contractor's estimate need to line up with the words in the adjuster's report, or payments get delayed. Ask your contractor to use clear labels for each phase, and keep copies of every document.

If the terms differ between your contractor and your carrier, ask each of them to explain what they mean by the term, and ask for the explanation in writing.

A short phone call among you, the contractor and the adjuster can clear up most of these mismatches before they turn into delays. Ask for the call early, and take notes.

State and local references

The Massachusetts Department of Public Health says moisture is the cause of mold, and that absorbent materials not dried within 24 to 48 hours often need to be replaced. That is the reason mitigation and drying come before anything else.

The Swansea Building Department says a permit is required for any installation or repair of plumbing, along with work such as re-roofing, electrical and mechanical systems. Repair work after a loss often triggers those requirements, so the person who rebuilds should be clear on permits.

Homeowners in Somerset and Rehoboth use the same terms on their quotes, and we explain each one over the phone.

Standards behind the words

Two published industry standards sit behind these terms. ANSI/IICRC S500 covers professional water damage restoration, and ANSI/IICRC S520 covers professional mold remediation. Knowing which one a contractor says it follows tells you what kind of work it has in mind.

What to do before you compare quotes

  • Ask each company which phase its quote covers
  • Do not compare totals without comparing scopes
  • Do not assume mitigation includes rebuilding
  • Ask whether mold work is included or separate
  • Keep the written scope from each company
